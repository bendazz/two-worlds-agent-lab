A TRAVELER'S HISTORY OF THE MEREHOLT
by Ansel Crewe, Keeper of the Lanterncross Archive

PREFACE

I, Ansel Crewe, have kept the Lanterncross Archive for twenty-two years. In that time I have been asked the same questions again and again by bargemen, envoys, students, salt merchants and children who wander in out of the rain. Where does the Sidd begin? Why will no one in Ost pick up a shovel? Who signed the treaty, and which treaty do you mean? This book is my attempt to answer those questions once, plainly, so that I may spend my remaining years answering new ones.

This book is written in the year TR 612. The letters TR stand for the Tidereckoning, the calendar used everywhere in the Mereholt. The Tidereckoning begins with the Great Tide, the flood that drowned the southern coast of the Mereholt and left the salt flats behind it. The year of the Great Tide is TR 1. Every date in this book is given in the Tidereckoning, and the reader should assume "TR" wherever a year appears.

A Traveler's History of the Mereholt is divided into three parts and two appendices. Part One describes the land itself, one region at a time: Ost in the north, the Vale of Siddow in the center, Hollin's Reach in the west, the Saltings on the south coast, and Varn with the Sedge Fens in the east. Part Two tells the history of the Mereholt in six eras, from the Unreckoned years to the present day. Part Three gathers short accounts of the men and women whose names a traveler will hear most often. The appendices give a timeline and a glossary.

A word of warning to the reader about names. The Mereholt is full of names that sound alike, and travelers confuse them constantly. Varn is a marsh town in the far east. Varne is a river-island city in the middle of the Vale. They are two different places, a long journey apart, and each gave its name to a different treaty. There were also two queens named Idrenne, who reigned centuries apart. I have tried to be careful with every one of these names, and I ask the reader to be careful too.

Ansel Crewe has not traveled every road in the Mereholt, and the Lanterncross Archive does not hold an answer to every question. Where the records are silent, I have said so rather than guess. A traveler is better served by an honest "no one knows" than by a confident story.

The Keeper of the Lanterncross Archive thanks the bargemen of the Sidd, the shepherds of Ost, the clerks of the Hollen Bank, and the salt-rakers of the Saltings, who fed me, corrected me, and laughed at me in roughly equal measure.

Ansel Crewe
Lanterncross, in the year TR 612

PART ONE: THE LAND

CHAPTER 1: OST

Ost is the northernmost region of the Mereholt. Ost is a high, treeless plateau, broad and windy, covered in short grass, heather and loose grey stone. A traveler climbing up from the Vale of Siddow feels the air grow thin and cold long before reaching the rim of the plateau. Snow lies on the high ground of Ost for much of the winter, and even in high summer the nights are cold enough to need a wool blanket.

The people of Ost are shepherds and poets. Nearly every family in Ost keeps sheep, and nearly every family in Ost can recite a hundred lines of verse from memory. The two callings are not separate in Ost. A shepherd walking behind a flock for a whole day has time to compose, and the long winter evenings in Ost are spent trading poems around the fire. The people of the Vale like to say that Ost exports only two things, wool and verses, and that the verses are worth more.

The sheep of Ost are a small, hardy, long-fleeced breed called greyback sheep. Greyback wool is the finest wool in the Mereholt. Weavers in Lanterncross pay a high price for it, and cloth made from greyback wool is called Ost cloth wherever it is sold. Every autumn the shepherds of Ost drive surplus sheep and carry bales of wool down to the Vale to trade for grain, which does not grow well on the plateau.

The most important custom in Ost is that digging is forbidden. The people of Ost do not mine, quarry, plough or dig wells. The custom is called the Unbroken Ground, and it is older than any written record in Ost. Travelers who expect a mountain people to be miners are always surprised to learn that the high country of the Mereholt has no mines at all. The people of Ost believe that the plateau is a single living thing and that a wound in the ground never heals. A shepherd in Ost will not even drive a tent peg; tents in Ost are held down with stones.

Because digging is forbidden in Ost, the houses of Ost are built from field stones gathered from the surface, fitted together without mortar. Stone gatherers in Ost walk the hills each spring collecting stones that the frost has pushed up out of the soil. A family house in Ost is low, round and thick-walled, with a roof of turf laid on top of timber brought up from the Vale. The dead of Ost are not buried; they are laid on the high ground and covered with a cairn of gathered stones.

Water in Ost comes from springs and from the many small streams that run across the plateau. Because the people of Ost dig no wells, every village in Ost is built beside a spring. The largest spring in Ost is the Weeping Stone, near the village of Harrowby. The streams of Ost gather together at the southern edge of the plateau.

The River Sidd rises in Ost. The streams of the plateau meet in a long hollow called the Gathering, and from the Gathering the Sidd pours over the southern rim of Ost and flows south, down into the Vale of Siddow. The falls where the Sidd leaves the plateau are called the Rimfalls. A traveler leaving Ost for the south usually follows the Sidd down the Rimfalls road and takes a barge once the river becomes calm enough to carry one.

The chief town of Ost is Brannock, which stands on the plateau above the Rimfalls. Brannock is not large, but it is where the wool fair of Ost is held every autumn, and it is where the Council of Shepherds meets. The Council of Shepherds settles disputes over grazing land and keeps the old boundaries between flocks. Brannock also has the only inn in Ost with more than six beds, the Fleece and Lantern.

Poetry in Ost is taken as seriously as law is taken elsewhere. Every midsummer the villages of Ost gather at Brannock for the Reckoning of Verses, a contest that lasts three days. Poets of Ost compete in long poems, short poems, and insult poems, and the winner of the long poem is given the title Voice of Ost for one year. The most famous poem ever composed in Ost is The Shepherd's Reckoning, by the poet Wenna Brask, which is described in Part Three of this book.

The people of Ost do not count themselves. No count of the population of Ost has ever been made, by the crown or by the Council of Shepherds, and the Lanterncross Archive holds no figure for it. The shepherds of Ost consider it unlucky to number people, although they number their sheep carefully. A traveler who asks how many people live in Ost will be told, politely, that the question is not answered on the plateau.

Food in Ost is plain and filling. The common dishes of Ost are mutton stew, hard sheep's cheese, and oatcakes made from grain carried up from the Vale. The people of Ost drink a thin sour milk called whey-ale. Fuel is scarce on a treeless plateau, and the people of Ost burn dried brushwood carried up from the Vale and dried sheep dung, which burns slowly and without much smoke.

Ost joined the Vale of Siddow to form a single realm under the Charter of Lanterncross, in TR 94. Ost has belonged to the realm ever since. The people of Ost kept the right to govern their own grazing through the Council of Shepherds, and the crown has never tried to overturn the custom of the Unbroken Ground. The Charter is described in Part Two, in the chapter on the Founding.

The year in Ost follows the flocks. Lambing in Ost begins in early spring, when the shepherds keep watch through the night in stone folds on the high ground. Shearing in Ost takes place at midsummer, just before the Reckoning of Verses, and the wool is packed in bales to wait for the autumn fair at Brannock. In late autumn the shepherds of Ost drive their flocks down from the highest ground to the sheltered southern slopes of the plateau, where the flocks spend the winter.

Every flock in Ost carries a mark. The shepherds of Ost mark their sheep with a dab of colored dye on the shoulder, and each family of Ost has its own color and pattern. The marks are recorded by the Council of Shepherds at Brannock, and it is a serious crime in Ost to change the mark on another family's sheep. Disputes over marks are settled by the Council of Shepherds, and the Council has settled such disputes for as long as there have been records in Ost.

Harrowby is the oldest village of Ost. Harrowby stands beside the Weeping Stone, the largest spring on the plateau, and it is said in Ost that every family on the plateau has at least one ancestor from Harrowby. The poet Wenna Brask grazed her flock on the slopes near Harrowby, and the people of Harrowby show visitors a flat rock where Wenna Brask is said to have sat while composing.

The weather of Ost is the hardest in the Mereholt. Wind blows across the plateau of Ost nearly every day of the year, and a still day in Ost is so rare that the people of the plateau call it a gift day and stop work to enjoy it. Travelers to Ost should bring a heavy cloak even in summer, and should never cross the open plateau alone in winter.

A traveler in Ost should remember three rules. The first rule in Ost is never to dig, not even a fire pit. The second rule in Ost is never to ask how many people live in a village. The third rule in Ost is to listen when someone begins to recite a poem, however long it is, because interrupting a poem is the rudest thing a guest can do on the plateau.

CHAPTER 2: THE VALE OF SIDDOW

The Vale of Siddow is the central region of the Mereholt. The Vale is a wide, low river valley of rich black soil, and it is the farmland of the realm. Most people simply call it the Vale. More people live in the Vale than in any other part of the Mereholt, and the capital of the realm stands in the middle of it.

The people of the Vale are grain farmers. The Vale grows wheat, barley and oats in long strips that run down to the river, and in a good year the Vale grows enough grain to feed the whole Mereholt. Harvest in the Vale begins in late summer and ends with the Sheaf Festival, when the last sheaf of each village is carried to the village hall and hung from the rafters until the next harvest. The farmers of the Vale are proud, practical people who measure a year by its harvest.

The Mereholt has two rivers, the Sidd and the Tamber, and both of them touch the Vale. The River Sidd enters the Vale from the north, after coming down from Ost, and flows south through the whole length of the Vale. The Sidd is wide, slow and deep in the Vale, and it carries most of the traffic of the realm. Grain barges, wool barges and passenger barges move along the Sidd every day of the year except in the coldest winters.

The capital of the Mereholt is Lanterncross. Lanterncross stands in the middle of the Vale, at the place where the River Sidd forks. At Lanterncross the Sidd divides into two branches, the Greater Sidd, which runs to the southeast, and the Lesser Sidd, which runs to the southwest. The city is built on both banks and on the point of land between the two branches, and it is joined together by eleven bridges.

Lanterncross takes its name from the great lanterns that stand on the point of land where the Sidd forks. The lanterns were first lit to guide barges through the fork at night, when the current is dangerous. Today there are nine lanterns on the point, and a keeper lights them every evening at dusk. The people of Lanterncross say that as long as the lanterns burn, the realm will stand.

Lanterncross is the seat of the crown. The Mereholt is a monarchy, ruled by queens and kings, and the monarch lives in the Hall of Lanterns on the point of land between the branches of the Sidd. The royal council meets in the Hall of Lanterns, and the Lanterncross Archive, which the author of this book keeps, occupies the old north wing of the Hall.

The markets of Lanterncross are the largest in the Mereholt. Grain from the Vale, wool from Ost, glass from Hollin's Reach, salt from the Saltings and eels from Varn all pass through the markets of Lanterncross. The greatest market is the Fork Market, held on the river quays every fifth day. Merchants in Lanterncross reckon prices in salt bricks and copper tallies, and larger sums in silver glass-marks.

Below Lanterncross, both branches of the Sidd continue south out of the Vale toward the Saltings. The Greater Sidd and the Lesser Sidd each carry barges south, and the barge trip from Lanterncross down to the edge of the Saltings is an easy one, since the river does the work. Barges returning north from the Saltings must be pulled by horses walking on towpaths along the banks.

The western edge of the Vale is the River Tamber. The Tamber is the second river of the Mereholt. The Tamber is narrower and faster than the Sidd, and it forms the border between the Vale of Siddow and Hollin's Reach. On the Vale side of the Tamber the land is open farmland; on the far side, the forest of Hollin's Reach begins almost at the water's edge. The Tamber flows south and joins the Lesser Sidd a little above the Saltings.

The eastern edge of the Vale is the beginning of the Sedge Fens. East of Lanterncross the farmland of the Vale becomes wetter and lower, the fields give way to water meadows, and at last the ground turns to reed and marsh. A traveler crossing the Vale from west to east walks through two full days of farmland on the Harvest Road, passing Lanterncross at the midpoint, before the road reaches the edge of the fens. The Sedge Fens are described in Chapter 5.

The city of Varne stands on an island in the River Sidd, in the northern part of the Vale, upstream of Lanterncross. Varne must not be confused with Varn, the marsh town in the far east of the Mereholt. Varne is a river-island city in the Vale; Varn is a fen town at the edge of the realm. The two names differ by a single letter, and travelers mix them up constantly.

Varne is a city of river toll collectors. Every barge that travels the Sidd between Ost and Lanterncross must pass Varne, because the river narrows on either side of the island, and the people of Varne have collected a toll on that traffic for as long as there have been barges. A chain can be raised across each channel at Varne, and no barge moves past the island until its toll is paid. The toll at Varne is paid in copper tallies, counted by the load.

The island of Varne is small and crowded. The whole city of Varne is built of brick, because there is no room on the island for anything but tall, narrow houses pressed close together. The streets of Varne are so narrow that two people carrying baskets cannot pass each other, and the people of Varne travel between houses by footbridges strung over the lanes. The Tollhouse of Varne, which stands at the northern tip of the island, is the oldest building in the city.

The toll collectors of Varne have a reputation in the Mereholt for honesty and for boredom. The families of Varne keep ledgers of every barge that has passed their island, and the oldest ledgers in Varne go back to the Founding era. The Lanterncross Archive has often borrowed the Varne ledgers to settle questions about trade in earlier centuries. In return, the city of Varne keeps a seat on the royal council.

Varne gave its name to the Treaty of Varne, which was signed on the island in TR 402. The Treaty of Varne brought Hollin's Reach into the realm. The Treaty of Varne is not the same as the Treaty of Varn, which ended the Salt War and was signed in the marsh town of Varn. Both treaties are described in Part Two.

The bargemen of the Sidd are a guild of their own. The Bargemen's Guild of Lanterncross licenses every barge that carries goods on the Sidd within the Vale, and a barge without the guild's painted mark on its bow may not load at the quays of Lanterncross. The bargemen of the Sidd wear blue caps and sing working songs as they pole and steer, and many of those songs are older than any written record in the Vale.

The Sidd floods in the Vale most springs, when the snow melts on the plateau of Ost. The spring flood of the Sidd spreads a thin layer of silt across the low fields beside the river, and that silt is the reason the soil of the Vale is so rich. The farmers of the Vale build their houses on low mounds above the flood line, and every village of the Vale keeps a flood bell to warn of high water.

The villages of the Vale are many and prosperous. The largest villages of the Vale are Tolsey, on the Greater Sidd south of Lanterncross; Marrowfield, on the Harvest Road east of Lanterncross; and Oakhurst Ferry, on the Tamber, where the main ferry crosses into Hollin's Reach. Each village of the Vale has a common granary, and the granaries of the Vale were the first thing the crown protected in times of famine.

The Vale of Siddow is the heart of the Mereholt, and it has always been the part of the realm most eager to rule the rest. The history of the Mereholt is in large part the history of the Vale trying, sometimes wisely and sometimes foolishly, to hold the other regions together. That history begins in Part Two.

CHAPTER 3: HOLLIN'S REACH

Hollin's Reach is the western region of the Mereholt. Hollin's Reach is an old forest of oak, beech and holly, so old that some of its trees are said to have been standing before the Great Tide. The name comes from the holly trees, which the old people of the west called hollin, and which grow thick beneath the taller trees all through the forest. Hollin's Reach is the only large forest in the Mereholt.

The people of Hollin's Reach are glassmakers and bankers. Travelers who expect a forest people to be hunters, archers or mystics are always disappointed. The people of Hollin's Reach do not hunt for their living, they are not famous with the bow, and they have no hidden wisdom of the trees. The people of Hollin's Reach make glass, and they lend money, and they are extremely good at both.

Glassmaking in Hollin's Reach depends on the forest. Glass furnaces need a great deal of fuel, and the forest of Hollin's Reach supplies it. The glassmakers of Hollin's Reach burn oak and beech in furnaces called glasshouses, and they make the ash of burned bracken into the potash that glass requires. Sand for the glass comes by barge from the banks of the Tamber. The glassmakers of Hollin's Reach cut and replant their woods in a careful rotation, so that the forest has never been exhausted in all the centuries of glassmaking.

The glass of Hollin's Reach is the finest in the Mereholt. Hollin's Reach makes window glass, drinking glass, lantern glass, spectacles, and colored glass for the windows of halls. The nine great lanterns of Lanterncross are glazed with glass from Hollin's Reach, and the glassmakers of the Reach replace a pane whenever one cracks, free of charge, by an old custom. The most prized glass of Hollin's Reach is a pale green glass called fernglass, whose recipe is kept secret by a single guild.

Banking in Hollin's Reach began with glass. The glassmakers of Hollin's Reach sold their glass all over the Mereholt and were often paid long after the glass was delivered, so they learned early to keep careful accounts, to lend, and to charge for waiting. Over time, the lending became more profitable than the glass. Today the banking houses of Hollin's Reach hold the savings of merchants from every region of the Mereholt, and the crown itself borrows from them.

The greatest bank of Hollin's Reach is the Hollen Bank, founded by Casimir Hollen. The Hollen Bank stands in the town of Emberlight, the chief town of Hollin's Reach. The Hollen Bank is a long, low building of timber and glass, and its counting room has a glass roof so that clerks can count coins by daylight without burning candles. Casimir Hollen is described in Part Three.

The silver glass-mark is the coin of Hollin's Reach, and it is used throughout the Mereholt for large sums. A silver glass-mark is a silver coin with a small bead of green glass set into its center, which makes it very hard to counterfeit. The value of money in the Mereholt is reckoned this way: 1 salt brick is worth 12 copper tallies, and 1 silver glass-mark is worth 5 salt bricks. The glass-marks are struck at the Hollen Bank in Emberlight.

Emberlight takes its name from the glasshouses, whose furnaces are never allowed to go out. At night the town of Emberlight glows orange through the trees, and a traveler approaching Emberlight after dark can see the light of the furnaces from miles away. Emberlight is built in a clearing of the forest, and its streets are paved with broken glass pressed into clay, which glitters underfoot.

The forest of Hollin's Reach is crossed by a small number of cart roads, called rides, cut straight through the trees. The main ride of Hollin's Reach runs east from Emberlight to the River Tamber. A traveler leaving Hollin's Reach for the east follows the ride to its end at the riverbank and crosses the Tamber into the Vale of Siddow by the ferry at Oakhurst Ferry. There is no bridge across the Tamber anywhere along the border of Hollin's Reach; every traveler crosses by ferry.

The glassmakers of Hollin's Reach are organized into four guilds. The Guild of Clear Glass makes window glass and lantern glass. The Guild of Vessels makes cups, bottles and bowls. The Guild of Lenses grinds spectacles and the reading glasses used by the clerks of the Lanterncross Archive. The Guild of Green Glass makes fernglass and keeps its recipe secret. The heads of the four guilds meet each spring in Emberlight to set the prices of glass for the year.

An apprentice glassmaker in Hollin's Reach serves for nine years before becoming a master. For the first three years an apprentice of Hollin's Reach does nothing but feed the furnace and sweep the glasshouse floor. An apprentice of Hollin's Reach is not allowed to touch hot glass until the fourth year. Every master glassmaker of Hollin's Reach keeps the first piece of glass he or she ever blew, however crooked it is.

The forest of Hollin's Reach is protected by the Forest Law. Under the Forest Law of Hollin's Reach, no tree may be cut without a license from the glass guilds, and for every tree that is cut, two must be planted. Foresters of Hollin's Reach walk the rides each day to check that the Forest Law is kept. The Forest Law is the reason Hollin's Reach is still a great forest after centuries of glassmaking.

The people of Hollin's Reach are reserved and careful. A banker of Hollin's Reach will listen to a proposal for an hour without speaking and then give an answer of one word. The people of Hollin's Reach dislike debt among themselves, even though they lend to everyone else, and it is said in Emberlight that a glassmaker would rather break a finished vase than owe a neighbor a copper tally.

Hollin's Reach was not always part of the Mereholt. For most of its history Hollin's Reach was an independent land, trading with the Vale across the Tamber but refusing to join the realm. Hollin's Reach joined the Mereholt through the Treaty of Varne, signed on the river island of Varne in TR 402, during the era called the Glass Peace. The Treaty of Varne is described in Part Two.

The food of Hollin's Reach is richer than the food of Ost. The people of Hollin's Reach eat pork from pigs fattened on acorns in the forest, mushrooms, chestnuts, and a dark bread made with chestnut flour. The drink of Hollin's Reach is a sweet cider made from the wild crab apples of the forest edge, served in green glass cups.

A traveler in Hollin's Reach should remember two rules. The first rule in Hollin's Reach is never to handle glass in a glasshouse without being asked. The second rule in Hollin's Reach is never to ask a banker of the Reach how much money is kept in the Hollen Bank. The bankers of Hollin's Reach will tell a customer exactly what the customer owns, and nothing else.

CHAPTER 4: THE SALTINGS

The Saltings are the southern coast of the Mereholt. The Saltings are a wide, flat country of salt flats, shallow pans and white crust, stretching for many miles between the southern edge of the Vale and the sea. The ground of the Saltings is so flat and so white that on a bright day a traveler must wear a veil of dark gauze to protect the eyes from the glare.

The salt flats of the Saltings were made by the Great Tide. In TR 1, the first year of the Tidereckoning, the Great Tide drowned the southern coast of the Mereholt. When the waters drew back, they left behind a broad plain soaked in seawater, and as the water dried, the salt stayed. The whole calendar of the Mereholt counts its years from that flood, and the people of the Saltings say, with some pride, that time itself began on their coast.

The people of the Saltings are salt harvesters. Salt-rakers of the Saltings flood shallow pans with brine, let the sun dry the brine, and rake up the salt that remains. The salt is then pressed into hard blocks called salt bricks, each stamped with the mark of the pan where it was made. Salt harvesting in the Saltings is summer work; in the winter the pans flood with rain and the salt-rakers mend their tools and their houses.

Salt bricks are money in the Mereholt. The salt brick began as a trade good and became the common coin of the realm, because every household needs salt and a salt brick keeps its value. The value of money in the Mereholt is reckoned this way: 1 salt brick is worth 12 copper tallies, and 1 silver glass-mark is worth 5 salt bricks. A salt brick used as money must carry the stamp of a licensed pan in the Saltings.

The Saltings have no navy. The Saltings are a coastal land, but the people of the Saltings distrust the sea and do not sail on it. The Saltings have no warships, no fishing fleet and no harbor for large ships. The people of the Saltings remember that the sea once drowned their coast, and they believe it will do so again if it is given the chance. A child of the Saltings is taught never to turn her back on the water and never to set foot in a boat that is not tied to a riverbank.

Because the people of the Saltings will not sail, all the trade of the Saltings travels over land and by river. Salt caravans leave the Saltings every week of the dry season, carrying salt bricks north on carts and on the backs of mules. The largest caravans of the Saltings travel along the Salt Road, which runs north beside the Greater Sidd. Barges also carry salt north up both branches of the Sidd, pulled along the towpaths by horses.

The River Sidd reaches the Saltings in two branches. The Greater Sidd and the Lesser Sidd flow south out of the Vale, spread out into many shallow channels across the Saltings, and finally reach the sea through a maze of mudbanks. The River Tamber has already joined the Lesser Sidd before it reaches the Saltings. The people of the Saltings build their villages on the raised banks of the river channels, which are the only ground in the Saltings high enough to stay dry in winter.

The chief town of the Saltings is Brinemouth, which stands where the Greater Sidd enters the salt flats. Brinemouth is a town of low white houses, built from blocks of salt-hardened clay and whitewashed every spring. The great salt market of the Saltings is held in Brinemouth, and every salt brick used as money is weighed at the Brinemouth Scales before it is stamped. Brinemouth faces inland, toward the river, and not one window in Brinemouth looks out at the sea.

The Saltings are governed by a Saltmistress. The Saltmistress is chosen by the heads of the salt-raking families and holds office for life. The Saltmistress speaks for the Saltings to the crown in Lanterncross, and since the end of the Salt War the Saltmistress has held a seat on the royal council. The most famous Saltmistress was Orla Dunmere, who led the Saltings during the Salt War and signed the Treaty of Varn. Orla Dunmere is described in Part Three.

The Saltings joined the realm in TR 203, when the salt-raking families accepted the protection of the crown in exchange for the right to choose their own Saltmistress. The peace between the Saltings and the Vale did not last. The Salt War, fought between the Saltings and the Vale over a tax on salt, began in TR 311 and ended in TR 318 with the Treaty of Varn. The Salt War is described in Part Two.

The salt pans of the Saltings are shallow square basins lined with packed clay. A single salt pan in the Saltings is worked by a single family, and the family's mark is pressed into every salt brick made from it. The largest salt-raking families of the Saltings work dozens of pans, and the oldest pans of the Saltings have been worked by the same families since the Founding.

The dark veils of the Saltings are woven from black goat hair and gauze. Every salt-raker of the Saltings wears a veil on the flats in summer, and a child of the Saltings receives a first veil at the age of six. The veil-makers of Brinemouth are among the most respected craftsmen in the Saltings, and a well-made veil is handed down from parent to child.

The food of the Saltings is salted, smoked or pickled. The people of the Saltings eat salt-cured mutton bought from Ost, pickled roots, and flat hard biscuits baked with coarse salt on top. The Saltings are famous for a sharp white cheese called brine-cheese, which is aged in barrels of salt water. The people of the Saltings do not eat fish from the sea, although they will eat eels from the fens of Varn.

The villages of the Saltings stand along the river channels, each on its own raised bank. The largest villages of the Saltings after Brinemouth are Graywell, on the Lesser Sidd, and Tallowmere, on the eastern edge of the flats. Every village of the Saltings keeps a salt store, a thick-walled building where salt bricks and salted food are kept dry through the winter rains.

The Saltings have a festival called the Turning of the Pans, held each year on the first dry day of summer, when the salt pans are flooded with brine for the first time. The whole of the Saltings walks the banks of the pans at dawn, and the Saltmistress pours the first bucket of brine. On the night of the Turning of the Pans, the people of the Saltings light fires along the riverbanks and keep their backs to the sea.

A traveler in the Saltings should remember three rules. The first rule in the Saltings is never to suggest a sea voyage. The second rule in the Saltings is never to break or chip a salt brick, which is like tearing a coin. The third rule in the Saltings is to wear a veil on the flats, because the glare of the Saltings has blinded more than one careless visitor.

CHAPTER 5: VARN AND THE SEDGE FENS

The Sedge Fens are the eastern marshes of the Mereholt. The Sedge Fens are a broad, wet country of reed beds, sedge meadows, slow black channels and shallow meres, lying east of the Vale of Siddow. The Sedge Fens take their name from the sedge, a tough, grass-like plant that covers the drier ground. From a distance the Sedge Fens look like a single flat green plain, but a traveler who leaves the path soon finds that much of that green is floating on water.

The Sedge Fens begin where the farmland of the Vale ends. A traveler coming from the Vale reaches the western edge of the Sedge Fens at the end of the Harvest Road, at a small village called Fenwatch. From Fenwatch, the only safe way east is the Causeway of Stakes, a raised path of packed earth and oak stakes built across the marsh. The Causeway of Stakes runs east through the Sedge Fens for a full day's walk and ends at the town of Varn.

Varn is the marsh town of the Sedge Fens and the easternmost town of the Mereholt. Varn stands on a low island of firm ground in the middle of the eastern fens, surrounded on every side by reed and water. Varn must not be confused with Varne, the river-island city in the Vale. Varn is a fen town in the far east; Varne is a city on the River Sidd. The two names differ by a single letter.

The people of Varn are eel fishers and reed weavers. Eels are the great harvest of the Sedge Fens. The eel fishers of Varn set wicker traps in the channels of the fens and empty them at dawn, and in the autumn, when the eels run, the whole town of Varn works through the night. Eels from Varn are smoked over smoldering reed and sold as far away as the Saltings, where they are one of the few foods from water that the people of the Saltings will eat.

Reed weaving is the second trade of Varn. The reed weavers of Varn cut the tall reeds of the Sedge Fens in winter, dry them, and weave them into mats, baskets, eel traps, screens and thatch. The roofs of half the villages in the Vale are thatched with reed from Varn. The finest work of the reed weavers of Varn is the fen-mat, a tightly woven floor mat with patterns of dyed reed, which is given as a wedding gift throughout the eastern Mereholt.

The houses of Varn are built on wooden piles driven into the soft ground, with floors raised above the level of the winter floods. The streets of Varn are boardwalks, and every house in Varn has a flat-bottomed punt tied to its door. The people of Varn travel through the Sedge Fens by punt, pushing the boat along the shallow channels with a long pole. A child of Varn learns to pole a punt before she learns to read.

The greatest building in Varn is the Moot Hall of Varn, a long hall of oak and thatch built on the highest ground of the town. The Moot Hall of Varn is where the people of Varn meet to settle their affairs. The Moot Hall of Varn is also where the Treaty of Varn was signed in TR 318, ending the Salt War. A carved oak table in the Moot Hall of Varn is still shown to visitors as the table on which the treaty was signed.

The Treaty of Varn was signed in Varn because Varn stood outside both of the warring sides. During the Salt War, the town of Varn belonged neither to the Vale nor to the Saltings, and both sides trusted the people of Varn to keep the peace talks safe. The Treaty of Varn was signed by Saltmistress Orla Dunmere for the Saltings and by Marshal Tobin Ashgrove for the Vale. The Treaty of Varn is described in Part Two, in the chapter on the Salt War.

Varn remained a free town, outside the realm, for many years after the Salt War. The Moot Hall of Varn voted to join the Mereholt in TR 470, after the crown helped rebuild the Causeway of Stakes following a great flood. Varn has a seat on the royal council today, just as Varne does, and the clerks of the royal council are careful to write the two names clearly.

The Sedge Fens are home to many birds. Herons, bitterns, marsh harriers and great flocks of geese live in the Sedge Fens, and in the spring the booming call of the bittern can be heard for miles across the reeds at dusk. The people of Varn call the bittern the fen-bull, and it is considered bad luck in Varn to harm one.

The fen-guides of Varn lead travelers who must leave the Causeway of Stakes. A fen-guide of Varn knows every channel, mere and floating reed bed within a day's poling of the town. The fen-guides of Varn are paid in smoked eels as often as in copper tallies, and a traveler who hires a fen-guide should expect to spend the night in a reed hut on a mud island.

The fens can be dangerous to travelers. Fog rises from the Sedge Fens almost every autumn morning, and a traveler who leaves the Causeway of Stakes in fog can walk into deep water without warning. The people of Varn hang bells on posts along the Causeway of Stakes, and on foggy days a bell-warden walks the causeway ringing each bell in turn so that travelers can follow the sound.

The town of Varn keeps its own measure for eels, called the Varn stick. A Varn stick is a length of polished oak marked with notches, and every eel sold in the market of Varn is laid against a Varn stick to set its price. The largest eel ever recorded in Varn is said to have reached the top notch of the Varn stick and is carved in outline on the door of the Moot Hall of Varn.

The food of Varn is eel in every form: smoked eel, eel pie, eel stew with fen herbs, and jellied eel, which visitors to Varn rarely finish. The people of Varn also eat duck, goose eggs and the roots of water plants, and they drink a bitter ale brewed with bog myrtle.

The chief festival of Varn is Eel Night, held on the first night of the autumn eel run. On Eel Night the people of Varn light lanterns in their punts and pole them out across the channels of the Sedge Fens, so that the whole marsh around Varn is dotted with floating lights. The day after Eel Night is a holiday in Varn, and very little work is done.

A traveler in the Sedge Fens should remember two rules. The first rule in the Sedge Fens is never to leave the Causeway of Stakes in fog. The second rule in the Sedge Fens is to say "Varn" and not "Varne" when speaking of the marsh town, because the people of Varn take the mistake as an insult.

PART TWO: THE HISTORY

CHAPTER 6: THE UNRECKONED YEARS

The Unreckoned years is the name the Mereholt gives to all the time before the Great Tide. The Unreckoned years are called unreckoned because no one counted them. In the Unreckoned years the land that would become the Mereholt was home to scattered clans, and those clans kept no calendar. There was no year one, no count of seasons, and no way of saying how long ago any event took place.

The clans of the Unreckoned years also kept no written records. The Lanterncross Archive holds no document, carving, ledger or letter from before the Great Tide, and no such document has ever been found anywhere in the Mereholt. Every written record in the realm begins after the Great Tide. The Keeper of the Lanterncross Archive has searched for older writing for twenty-two years and has found none.

Because the clans of the Unreckoned years had neither a calendar nor writing, almost nothing is known about them. The Lanterncross Archive cannot say how many clans there were, what they were called, who led them, or how long the Unreckoned years lasted. It is not known where the clans lived, what they believed, or whether they fought or traded with one another. The honest answer to nearly every question about the Unreckoned years is that no one knows.

Travelers will hear stories about the Unreckoned years in every tavern of the Mereholt. The shepherds of Ost have their tales, the bargemen of the Sidd have theirs, and the salt-rakers of the Saltings have theirs, and no two stories agree. This book does not repeat those stories. None of them can be traced to any record, and the Lanterncross Archive treats them as entertainment and not as history.

The Unreckoned years ended with the Great Tide. The Great Tide drowned the southern coast of the Mereholt and left the salt flats of the Saltings behind it. The people of the Mereholt chose that flood as the beginning of their calendar, and the year of the Great Tide became TR 1, the first year of the Tidereckoning. Everything this book can say with confidence about the history of the Mereholt happened in or after TR 1.

The name Tidereckoning means "the reckoning from the Tide." Every date in the Mereholt is counted from the Great Tide, and a year is written with the letters TR before the number. There is no count of years backward from TR 1. A person of the Mereholt who wishes to speak of the time before the Great Tide simply says "in the Unreckoned years."

CHAPTER 7: THE FOUNDING

The Founding is the first era of the Tidereckoning. The Founding began with the Great Tide in TR 1 and ended in TR 180. During the Founding, the scattered people of the Vale and the plateau became a single realm, the calendar was adopted, and the first written records of the Mereholt were made.

The Founding began with the Great Tide. In TR 1 the Great Tide drowned the southern coast of the Mereholt. The people who lived near the coast fled north, up the valley of the River Sidd, into the land that is now the Vale of Siddow. When the water drew back, it left the salt flats that would become the Saltings. The survivors of the Great Tide began counting years from the flood, and the Tidereckoning was born.

The early Founding was a time of resettlement. The refugees from the drowned coast and the older people of the Vale cleared the valley floor for farming and built villages along the Sidd. The richest ground lay at the fork of the Sidd, and a settlement grew up on the point of land between the two branches. That settlement became Lanterncross, the capital of the Mereholt.

Writing came into common use in the Vale during the Founding. The oldest written records in the Mereholt are ledgers kept by the toll collectors of Varne, the river-island city in the Vale, who recorded the barges passing their island. The oldest surviving page of the Varne ledgers dates from TR 58. The Lanterncross Archive holds copies of those early ledgers.

During the Founding, the Vale of Siddow and the plateau of Ost traded wool for grain but were governed separately. The villages of the Vale were ruled by their own headmen, and the shepherds of Ost were ruled by the Council of Shepherds. Raids and quarrels over grazing on the slopes between the two regions were common in the first century of the Tidereckoning.

The Charter of Lanterncross united Ost and the Vale of Siddow into a single realm. The Charter of Lanterncross was agreed in TR 94, in the settlement at the fork of the Sidd. The headmen of the Vale and the Council of Shepherds of Ost agreed to be ruled by one crown, with the settlement at the fork of the Sidd as its seat. The Charter of Lanterncross is the founding document of the Mereholt, and the original copy is kept in the Lanterncross Archive.

The Charter of Lanterncross made the Mereholt a monarchy. Under the Charter, the realm is ruled by a queen or king, who holds the crown for life and passes it to an heir chosen from the royal family. The Charter also protected the customs of Ost, including the Unbroken Ground, and gave the Council of Shepherds the right to govern grazing on the plateau.

The first sovereign under the Charter of Lanterncross was Queen Idrenne Vosk. Queen Idrenne Vosk was crowned in TR 94, the same year the Charter was agreed, at the age of 23. Queen Idrenne Vosk had been chosen by the headmen of the Vale and the Council of Shepherds together, because she was trusted on both sides: she was a daughter of the Vale who had spent her childhood among the flocks of Ost. Queen Idrenne Vosk reigned until her death in TR 131.

Queen Idrenne Vosk's reign set the pattern for the realm. Queen Idrenne Vosk built the first lanterns on the point at the fork of the Sidd, gave the city of Lanterncross its name, and began the royal council. Queen Idrenne Vosk also set the first fixed toll at Varne, which ended the quarrels between the bargemen and the toll collectors of the island.

Queen Idrenne Vosk's royal council was small. The first royal council of the Mereholt had nine members: six headmen from the villages of the Vale and three shepherds chosen by the Council of Shepherds of Ost. The royal council met in a timber hall on the point at the fork of the Sidd until the Hall of Lanterns was built.

The later Founding was a time of steady growth. The Vale was cleared from the Tamber in the west to the edge of the Sedge Fens in the east, and the Harvest Road was built across it. The salt of the new flats in the south began to be raked and carried north for trade, and the first salt bricks were pressed.

The Founding ended in TR 180, when the Hall of Lanterns was completed on the point of land at the fork of the Sidd. The Hall of Lanterns became the seat of the crown and the meeting place of the royal council. By the end of the Founding, the Mereholt was a single realm of two regions, Ost and the Vale of Siddow, with Hollin's Reach, the Saltings and Varn still outside it.

CHAPTER 8: THE SALT WAR

The Salt War was the great war of the Mereholt. The Salt War was fought between the Vale of Siddow and the Saltings. The Salt War began in TR 311 and ended in TR 318, when it was brought to a close by the Treaty of Varn.

The years before the Salt War were years of growing trade. The Saltings joined the realm in TR 203, when the salt-raking families accepted the protection of the crown in exchange for the right to choose their own Saltmistress. For the next century, salt from the Saltings flowed north up the Sidd to Lanterncross, and salt bricks became the common money of the realm.

The cause of the Salt War was a tax on salt. In TR 310 the royal council in Lanterncross, which was controlled by the great farming families of the Vale, passed the Salt Levy. The Salt Levy required the Saltings to hand over one salt brick in every four to the crown. Because salt bricks were money, the Salt Levy was a tax on the money of the Saltings itself, and the salt-raking families saw it as theft.

The Saltings refused to pay the Salt Levy. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere, speaking for the salt-raking families, declared that the Saltings would not hand over a single brick. When the collectors of the crown arrived at Brinemouth in the spring of TR 311, the people of the Saltings turned them away at the edge of the flats. The royal council sent soldiers to collect the tax by force, and the Salt War began in TR 311.

The Vale was led in the Salt War by Marshal Tobin Ashgrove. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove commanded the soldiers of the crown, who were mostly farmers of the Vale called up from their villages. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove was a careful commander who disliked the war from the beginning, but he served the royal council faithfully.

The Saltings were led in the Salt War by Saltmistress Orla Dunmere. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere had no army at the start of the war. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere armed the salt-rakers with rakes, poles and slings, and she used the flat, blinding ground of the Saltings as her fortress.

The Salt War was fought entirely on land. The Saltings had no navy, and the people of the Saltings would not fight on the sea. The soldiers of the Vale could not use the sea either, because the Saltings coast has no harbor. Every battle of the Salt War was fought on the salt flats, along the river channels, or on the roads between the Saltings and the Vale.

The chief weapon of the Saltings was the salt itself. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere stopped every salt caravan and every salt barge from leaving the Saltings. Without new salt bricks, money grew scarce in the Vale, prices rose in Lanterncross, and the farmers of the Vale could not salt their meat for the winter. The stopping of the salt, which the people of the Vale called the Salt Famine, did more harm to the Vale than any battle.

The largest battle of the Salt War was the Battle of the White Flats, fought in TR 314 on the salt flats south of Brinemouth. The soldiers of the Vale marched onto the flats in bright sunshine, and many of them were blinded by the glare. The salt-rakers of the Saltings, wearing their dark veils, drove them back to the river. After the Battle of the White Flats, the Vale never again tried to take the flats by force.

The Salt War divided families across the realm. Many salt-raking families of the Saltings had cousins among the bargemen and farmers of the Vale, and during the Salt War letters between them were carried secretly by the eel traders of Varn, who moved freely between both sides. Some of those letters survive in the Lanterncross Archive, and they show that ordinary people on both sides of the Salt War wanted it to end long before their leaders did.

The shepherds of Ost took no side in the Salt War. The Council of Shepherds of Ost declared that the quarrel over salt was a quarrel of the Vale, and no shepherd of Ost was called up to fight. The people of Ost continued to trade wool for grain throughout the Salt War, and they bought salt from smugglers at a high price.

The Salt War ended in a stalemate. The Vale could not conquer the Saltings, and the Saltings could not live forever without grain from the Vale. By TR 317 both sides were exhausted, and Marshal Tobin Ashgrove persuaded the royal council to seek peace.

The Treaty of Varn ended the Salt War. The Treaty of Varn was signed in TR 318 in the Moot Hall of Varn, the marsh town in the Sedge Fens. The peace talks were held in Varn because Varn was then a free town that belonged to neither side. The Treaty of Varn was signed by Saltmistress Orla Dunmere for the Saltings and by Marshal Tobin Ashgrove for the Vale. The Treaty of Varn must not be confused with the Treaty of Varne, a different treaty signed on the river island of Varne in TR 402.

The Treaty of Varn had three terms. The first term of the Treaty of Varn abolished the Salt Levy forever. The second term of the Treaty of Varn gave the Saltmistress a permanent seat on the royal council, so that the Saltings would never again be taxed without a voice. The third term of the Treaty of Varn gave the Saltings sole right to stamp salt bricks as money, which is why every salt brick is still weighed at the Brinemouth Scales.

The Salt War left a lasting mark on the Mereholt. Since the Treaty of Varn, no ruler of the Mereholt has taxed salt, and the saying "as foolish as the Salt Levy" is still used in Lanterncross for any law that hurts the people who pass it. The Salt War ended in TR 318, and the long peace that followed is described in the next chapter.

CHAPTER 9: THE GLASS PEACE

The Glass Peace is the era that followed the Salt War. The Glass Peace began in TR 318, with the signing of the Treaty of Varn, and lasted until TR 446, the year before the Long Frost. The Glass Peace takes its name from Hollin's Reach, whose glass and whose banks came to shape the whole realm during those years.

The Glass Peace was the longest time of peace in the history of the Mereholt. During the Glass Peace no war was fought between the regions of the realm, and the Saltmistress sat on the royal council beside the great families of the Vale, as the Treaty of Varn required. Trade on the River Sidd grew every year, and the toll ledgers of Varne from the Glass Peace are the thickest in the island's archive.

At the start of the Glass Peace, Hollin's Reach was still outside the realm. Hollin's Reach was an independent land of glassmakers and bankers on the western bank of the River Tamber. The people of Hollin's Reach traded freely with the Vale, but they had always refused to live under the crown in Lanterncross.

The Hollen Bank was founded during the Glass Peace. Casimir Hollen, a glassmaker of Emberlight, founded the Hollen Bank in TR 355. Casimir Hollen had grown rich selling lantern glass to the cities of the Vale, and he began lending that wealth to merchants on both sides of the Tamber. Within a generation the Hollen Bank was lending to the crown of the Mereholt itself.

The debts of the crown drew Hollin's Reach and the Vale together. By the end of the fourth century of the Tidereckoning, the crown in Lanterncross owed the Hollen Bank more than it could easily repay, and the Hollen Bank depended on the crown to protect its loans to merchants of the Vale. The bankers of Hollin's Reach began to see that their money would be safer inside the realm than outside it.

The Treaty of Varne brought Hollin's Reach into the realm of the Mereholt. The Treaty of Varne was signed in TR 402 on the river island of Varne, in the Tollhouse of Varne. The Treaty of Varne was signed by Queen Idrenne the Younger for the crown, and by the heads of the glass guilds of Hollin's Reach for the Reach. The Treaty of Varne is not the Treaty of Varn; the Treaty of Varn ended the Salt War and was signed in the marsh town of Varn in TR 318.

The Treaty of Varne was signed on Varne because the island was a meeting place both sides respected. The toll collectors of Varne had kept careful records of river trade for centuries, and the envoys of both sides trusted the Varne ledgers. The envoys of Hollin's Reach traveled to Varne by river, and Queen Idrenne the Younger traveled up the Sidd from Lanterncross.

Queen Idrenne the Younger is not the same person as Queen Idrenne Vosk. Queen Idrenne Vosk was the first sovereign under the Charter of Lanterncross, crowned in TR 94. Queen Idrenne the Younger reigned centuries later and was named in honor of the first queen. Queen Idrenne the Younger is remembered as the queen who made the Mereholt whole in the west.

The Treaty of Varne had three terms. The first term of the Treaty of Varne made Hollin's Reach a region of the realm, under the crown in Lanterncross. The second term of the Treaty of Varne let the glass guilds of Hollin's Reach keep their own laws of trade and banking. The third term of the Treaty of Varne gave the Hollen Bank the right to strike a coin for the whole realm.

The silver glass-mark was first struck in TR 409, under the third term of the Treaty of Varne. The silver glass-mark was made for large sums, which were awkward to pay in salt bricks. The rates of the realm's money were fixed at the same time: 1 salt brick is worth 12 copper tallies, and 1 silver glass-mark is worth 5 salt bricks. Those rates have not changed since the Glass Peace.

The Glass Peace was also a time of learning. Reading and writing spread from the clerks of Lanterncross to the merchants and bargemen of the Vale during the Glass Peace, because trade with the Hollen Bank required written accounts. The glass guilds of Hollin's Reach sold spectacles to anyone who could pay, and it is said in the Vale that the Glass Peace made the whole realm able to read.

The Glass Peace was a time of building. The great ferry at Oakhurst Ferry was built across the Tamber during the Glass Peace, the Harvest Road was paved with stone as far as Fenwatch, and the glaziers of Hollin's Reach glazed the nine great lanterns of Lanterncross. Much of what a traveler sees in Lanterncross today was built during the Glass Peace.

The Glass Peace ended in TR 446. The next year, TR 447, brought the first winter of the Long Frost, which is described in the next chapter.

CHAPTER 10: THE LONG FROST

The Long Frost was the great disaster of the Mereholt. The Long Frost lasted from TR 447 to TR 449. During the Long Frost the winters were so hard and the summers so short that the River Sidd froze and the grain harvest of the Vale failed.

The Long Frost began in TR 447. The winter of TR 447 came early, and snow fell on the Vale of Siddow before the harvest was in. The frost did not lift in the spring. Summer in the Long Frost lasted only a few weeks, and the grain in the fields of the Vale never ripened.

The River Sidd froze during the Long Frost. The River Sidd had frozen at its edges in hard winters before, but in the Long Frost the Sidd froze from bank to bank, from the Rimfalls to Lanterncross. The ice on the Sidd was thick enough for carts to cross. No barge moved on the Sidd for months at a time, and the toll collectors of Varne recorded no traffic at all for the whole winter of TR 448.

The frozen Sidd cut the realm apart. Because the Sidd carried most of the trade of the Mereholt, the freezing of the river stopped the barges. Grain, wool and glass could move between the regions only in small loads, by sledge and cart over the ice, so little food reached Ost from Lanterncross and little wool reached Lanterncross from Ost.

The grain harvest of the Vale failed in TR 447 and again in TR 448. The Vale of Siddow, which in a good year feeds the whole Mereholt, could not feed even itself. The common granaries of the Vale were emptied in the first winter, and by the second winter the people of the Vale were starving.

The Saltings fed the Vale during the Long Frost. The Saltings had never depended on the harvest in the same way as the Vale. The people of the Saltings had great stores of salted mutton, pickled roots, brine-cheese and salt biscuits, all of which keep for years. When the grain of the Vale failed, the Saltmistress opened the salt stores of the Saltings and sent food north to the Vale.

The food of the Saltings reached the Vale by caravan. With the Sidd frozen, barges could not carry food north, so the salt caravans of the Saltings carried it overland along the Salt Road. The caravans of the Saltings traveled through snow for weeks at a time, and many salt-rakers died on the road. The people of the Vale have never forgotten that the Saltings, against whom they had fought the Salt War, saved them in the Long Frost.

Ost suffered badly in the Long Frost. The flocks of Ost could not graze on the frozen plateau, and many greyback sheep died. Because the custom of the Unbroken Ground forbids digging, the people of Ost could not break the frozen ground to plant anything, and they depended on food carried up from the south. The poets of Ost made many poems about the Long Frost, and the saddest of them are still recited at Brannock.

The eel fishers of Varn also helped during the Long Frost. The channels of the Sedge Fens froze, but the eel fishers of Varn cut holes in the ice and kept fishing. Smoked eel from Varn was carried west across the frozen fens and sold in Lanterncross, where it was eaten by people who had never tasted eel before.

The crown did what it could during the Long Frost. The royal council in Lanterncross opened the royal stores, forbade the sale of grain outside the Vale, and sent sledges across the frozen Sidd to carry food to the villages cut off by snow. The Hollen Bank of Hollin's Reach lent the crown money to buy food from the Saltings and did not ask for repayment until the harvest returned.

The Long Frost ended in TR 449. The spring of TR 449 came late but warm, and the ice on the River Sidd broke up in a great flood that swept away two bridges at Lanterncross. The harvest of TR 449 was small but it ripened, and the Vale began to recover.

The Long Frost changed the Mereholt. After the Long Frost, the crown built new royal granaries at Lanterncross and Tolsey and kept them full, and the Saltmistress was given the honor of opening the Sheaf Festival in Lanterncross every year. The people of the Mereholt still say "salt before grain" to mean that an old friend is worth more than a good harvest.

CHAPTER 11: THE PRESENT

The Present is the name this book gives to the era from TR 450 to TR 612, the year in which this book is written. The Present began after the end of the Long Frost and continues today. The Present has been an era of recovery, growth and, for the most part, peace.

The first task of the Present was recovery from the Long Frost. The farms of the Vale were replanted, the flocks of Ost were rebuilt, and the bridges of Lanterncross that were swept away when the ice broke were rebuilt in stone. By TR 460 the Vale of Siddow was once again growing enough grain to feed the whole realm.

Varn joined the realm during the Present. In TR 468 a great spring flood broke the Causeway of Stakes in many places, cutting the marsh town of Varn off from the Vale. The crown sent workers and oak timber to rebuild the Causeway of Stakes, and in gratitude the Moot Hall of Varn voted to join the Mereholt in TR 470. Since TR 470, Varn has been a town of the realm, and every region of the Mereholt has lived under one crown.

The Mereholt in the Present has five regions: Ost, the Vale of Siddow, Hollin's Reach, the Saltings, and Varn with the Sedge Fens. The capital of the Mereholt is Lanterncross. The Mereholt is a monarchy ruled by a queen or king, as it has been since the Charter of Lanterncross.

The royal council of the Present has more members than ever before. The royal council includes the great families of the Vale, a speaker for the Council of Shepherds of Ost, the Saltmistress of the Saltings, a banker of the Hollen Bank for Hollin's Reach, and delegates from Varne and from Varn. The clerks of the royal council write the names of Varne and Varn in different colored inks to avoid confusion.

The Present has been an age of poetry in Ost. The most famous poet of the Mereholt, Wenna Brask of Ost, lived in the Present and composed The Shepherd's Reckoning, which is now recited at the Reckoning of Verses in Brannock every midsummer. Wenna Brask is described in Part Three.

The Lanterncross Archive was founded during the Present. The Lanterncross Archive was founded in TR 512 in the old north wing of the Hall of Lanterns, to gather the records of every region in one place. The Archive holds the original Charter of Lanterncross, copies of the Treaty of Varn and the Treaty of Varne, and copies of the oldest Varne toll ledgers.

The reigning monarch of the Mereholt at the time of writing is Queen Marit Sollace. Queen Marit Sollace was crowned in TR 598. Queen Marit Sollace is known for her interest in roads, and in her reign the Salt Road has been paved as far as Brinemouth.

Trade in the Present is greater than ever before. Glass from Hollin's Reach is sold in every village of the realm, wool from Ost is woven in Lanterncross, eels from Varn are eaten in the Saltings, and salt bricks from the Saltings are spent in every market. The Fork Market of Lanterncross has grown so large in the Present that it now spreads across both banks of the Sidd.

There has been no war between the regions of the Mereholt during the Present. The only armed trouble of the Present was a quarrel over grazing land on the slopes below Ost in TR 541, called the Fence Quarrel, which was settled by the Council of Shepherds without bloodshed.

The Present has brought new festivals to the Mereholt. Since the Long Frost, the Saltmistress of the Saltings has opened the Sheaf Festival in Lanterncross every year by breaking a loaf of bread baked with salt from the Saltings and flour from the Vale. Since TR 470, the people of Varn have sent a boat of smoked eels to the Fork Market of Lanterncross on the day after Eel Night, and the eels are given away free to anyone who asks.

Travel in the Mereholt of the Present is by river barge and by caravan, as it always has been. Barges carry most goods and most travelers along the Sidd, and caravans carry salt and other goods along the Salt Road and the Harvest Road. A traveler in the Present can cross the whole realm without meeting a soldier or paying any toll except the toll at Varne.

This book, A Traveler's History of the Mereholt, is written in TR 612 by Ansel Crewe, Keeper of the Lanterncross Archive. What follows in Part Three are short accounts of the people whose names a traveler will hear most often in the Mereholt of the Present.

PART THREE: NOTABLE FIGURES

CHAPTER 12: NOTABLE FIGURES

This chapter gives short accounts of six people whose names a traveler will hear most often in the Mereholt. The six are Queen Idrenne Vosk, Queen Idrenne the Younger, Marshal Tobin Ashgrove, Saltmistress Orla Dunmere, Casimir Hollen, and Wenna Brask. Each account names its subject in full, so that no reader confuses one person with another.

Queen Idrenne Vosk was the first sovereign of the Mereholt under the Charter of Lanterncross. Queen Idrenne Vosk was crowned in TR 94, the year the Charter of Lanterncross united Ost and the Vale of Siddow, and she was 23 years old at her crowning. Queen Idrenne Vosk was a daughter of a farming family of the Vale who had spent her childhood among the shepherds of Ost, and both regions trusted her for that reason.

Queen Idrenne Vosk reigned from TR 94 until her death in TR 131. Queen Idrenne Vosk founded the royal council, lit the first lanterns at the fork of the Sidd, and set the first fixed toll at Varne. Queen Idrenne Vosk is buried, by her own wish, under a cairn of gathered stones on the plateau of Ost, in the manner of the shepherds. The people of the Mereholt call Queen Idrenne Vosk "the First Queen."

Queen Idrenne Vosk is remembered for her fairness. A story told in both Ost and the Vale says that Queen Idrenne Vosk once settled a quarrel between a farmer of the Vale and a shepherd of Ost by making each of them spend a season doing the other's work. The Lanterncross Archive cannot confirm the story, but the saying "a season in the other's boots" comes from it and is still used across the Mereholt.

Queen Idrenne the Younger was the queen who signed the Treaty of Varne. Queen Idrenne the Younger reigned in the fifth century of the Tidereckoning and was named in honor of Queen Idrenne Vosk, the First Queen. Queen Idrenne the Younger is a different person from Queen Idrenne Vosk, and the two reigned centuries apart.

Queen Idrenne the Younger signed the Treaty of Varne in TR 402, on the river island of Varne, bringing Hollin's Reach into the realm. Queen Idrenne the Younger had spent many years negotiating with the glass guilds of Hollin's Reach, and she is said to have learned to blow glass during her visits to Emberlight. Queen Idrenne the Younger is remembered as the queen who made the Mereholt whole in the west. Queen Idrenne the Younger never visited the marsh town of Varn.

Marshal Tobin Ashgrove led the soldiers of the Vale in the Salt War, which began in TR 311 and ended in TR 318. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove came from a family of grain farmers near Tolsey and rose through the royal guard to command the crown's soldiers. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove opposed the Salt Levy in the royal council before the war, but when the council chose war, he obeyed.

Marshal Tobin Ashgrove is remembered for ending the Salt War more than for fighting it. After the defeat of the Vale at the Battle of the White Flats, Marshal Tobin Ashgrove argued in the royal council for peace. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove traveled to the marsh town of Varn and signed the Treaty of Varn in TR 318 on behalf of the Vale. Marshal Tobin Ashgrove retired to his family's farm near Tolsey after the war.

Saltmistress Orla Dunmere led the Saltings in the Salt War. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere was chosen as Saltmistress by the salt-raking families of the Saltings in TR 305 and held the office for the rest of her life. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere refused the Salt Levy, turned away the crown's collectors at Brinemouth, and armed the salt-rakers of the Saltings with rakes, poles and slings.

Saltmistress Orla Dunmere fought the Vale to a standstill and won only one battle. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere stopped the salt caravans and salt barges from leaving the Saltings, which caused the Salt Famine in the Vale. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere's salt-rakers won the Battle of the White Flats in TR 314. Saltmistress Orla Dunmere signed the Treaty of Varn in TR 318 in the Moot Hall of Varn, for the Saltings, and became the first Saltmistress to sit on the royal council.

Saltmistress Orla Dunmere is honored in the Saltings above every other Saltmistress. A statue of Saltmistress Orla Dunmere, carved from white stone and wearing a veil, stands in the market square of Brinemouth, facing north toward the Vale and away from the sea. The salt-rakers of the Saltings leave a pinch of salt at the feet of the statue on the anniversary of the Treaty of Varn.

Casimir Hollen was the founder of the Hollen Bank. Casimir Hollen was a glassmaker of Emberlight in Hollin's Reach who grew rich selling lantern glass to the cities of the Vale. Casimir Hollen founded the Hollen Bank in TR 355, during the Glass Peace. The Hollen Bank is now the greatest bank in the Mereholt.

Casimir Hollen is remembered in Hollin's Reach as a sharp man of few words. Casimir Hollen is said to have answered every request for a loan with either "yes" or "no" and nothing more. Casimir Hollen did not live to see the Treaty of Varne, but the loans of the Hollen Bank to the crown were one of the reasons Hollin's Reach joined the realm. The town of Emberlight holds a feast in Casimir Hollen's name every winter, at which no one is allowed to speak more than one word at a time.

Wenna Brask was a poet of Ost and the most famous poet of the Mereholt. Wenna Brask was a shepherd of the plateau of Ost, near the village of Harrowby, and she composed her poems while walking behind her flock. Wenna Brask lived in the Present era and won the title Voice of Ost at the Reckoning of Verses in Brannock four times.

Wenna Brask's greatest work is The Shepherd's Reckoning. The Shepherd's Reckoning is a long poem about a single year in the life of a shepherd of Ost, from the spring lambing to the winter fires, and it is written in a hundred and twelve verses. The Shepherd's Reckoning is recited in full at the Reckoning of Verses in Brannock every midsummer, and many people of Ost know the whole poem by heart. Wenna Brask asked that no copy of The Shepherd's Reckoning be written down in Ost, but a copy is kept in the Lanterncross Archive.

APPENDIX A: TIMELINE

All years are given in the Tidereckoning (TR).

Before TR 1: The Unreckoned years. Scattered clans, with no calendar and no written records.
TR 1: The Great Tide drowns the southern coast and leaves the salt flats of the Saltings. The Tidereckoning begins.
TR 1 to TR 180: The Founding.
TR 58: The oldest surviving page of the Varne toll ledgers.
TR 94: The Charter of Lanterncross unites Ost and the Vale of Siddow. Queen Idrenne Vosk is crowned, at the age of 23.
TR 131: Death of Queen Idrenne Vosk.
TR 180: The Hall of Lanterns is completed in Lanterncross. End of the Founding.
TR 203: The Saltings join the realm.
TR 305: Orla Dunmere is chosen Saltmistress of the Saltings.
TR 310: The royal council passes the Salt Levy.
TR 311 to TR 319: The Salt War, between the Vale of Siddow and the Saltings, ended by the Treaty of Varn.
TR 314: The Battle of the White Flats.
TR 355: Casimir Hollen founds the Hollen Bank in Emberlight.
TR 402: The Treaty of Varne, signed on the island of Varne by Queen Idrenne the Younger. Hollin's Reach joins the realm.
TR 409: The first silver glass-marks are struck.
TR 447 to TR 449: The Long Frost. The River Sidd freezes and the grain of the Vale fails.
TR 450: The Present begins.
TR 468: A spring flood breaks the Causeway of Stakes.
TR 470: Varn joins the realm.
TR 512: The Lanterncross Archive is founded.
TR 541: The Fence Quarrel below Ost.
TR 598: Queen Marit Sollace is crowned.
TR 612: This book is written.

APPENDIX B: GLOSSARY

Battle of the White Flats: The largest battle of the Salt War, fought in TR 314 on the salt flats south of Brinemouth and won by the Saltings.
Brannock: The chief town of Ost, above the Rimfalls, where the wool fair and the Reckoning of Verses are held.
Brine-cheese: A sharp white cheese of the Saltings, aged in barrels of salt water.
Brinemouth: The chief town of the Saltings, where the Greater Sidd enters the salt flats.
Brinemouth Scales: The scales at Brinemouth where every salt brick used as money is weighed before it is stamped.
Causeway of Stakes: The raised path through the Sedge Fens from Fenwatch to Varn.
Charter of Lanterncross: The founding document of the Mereholt, agreed in TR 94, which united Ost and the Vale of Siddow under one crown.
Copper tally: The smallest coin of the Mereholt. 12 copper tallies are worth 1 salt brick.
Council of Shepherds: The council of Ost that governs grazing on the plateau, meeting at Brannock.
Emberlight: The chief town of Hollin's Reach, home of the Hollen Bank and the glasshouses.
Fenwatch: The village at the western edge of the Sedge Fens, at the end of the Harvest Road.
Fernglass: A pale green glass of Hollin's Reach whose recipe is kept secret by a single guild.
Fork Market: The great market of Lanterncross, held on the river quays every fifth day.
Great Tide: The flood that drowned the southern coast of the Mereholt and left the salt flats behind. The Great Tide marks TR 1.
Greater Sidd: The southeastern branch of the River Sidd below Lanterncross.
Greyback sheep: The small, long-fleeced sheep of Ost.
Hall of Lanterns: The seat of the crown in Lanterncross, completed in TR 180.
Harvest Road: The road that crosses the Vale of Siddow from west to east.
Hollen Bank: The greatest bank of the Mereholt, in Emberlight, founded by Casimir Hollen in TR 355.
Hollin's Reach: The forest region in the west of the Mereholt, home of glassmakers and bankers.
Lanterncross: The capital of the Mereholt, where the River Sidd forks.
Lesser Sidd: The southwestern branch of the River Sidd below Lanterncross.
Long Frost: The great disaster of TR 447 to TR 449, when the River Sidd froze and the grain of the Vale failed.
Moot Hall of Varn: The meeting hall of the marsh town of Varn, where the Treaty of Varn was signed.
Oakhurst Ferry: The village and ferry on the River Tamber between the Vale of Siddow and Hollin's Reach.
Ost: The high, treeless plateau in the north of the Mereholt, home of shepherds and poets.
Reckoning of Verses: The poetry contest held at Brannock every midsummer.
Rimfalls: The falls where the River Sidd leaves the plateau of Ost.
Salt brick: A pressed block of salt used as money. 1 salt brick is worth 12 copper tallies.
Salt Levy: The tax on salt passed by the royal council in TR 310, which caused the Salt War.
Salt Road: The caravan road that runs north from the Saltings beside the Greater Sidd.
Salt War: The war between the Vale of Siddow and the Saltings, from TR 311 to TR 318.
Saltings: The salt flats of the southern coast of the Mereholt.
Saltmistress: The leader of the Saltings, chosen for life by the salt-raking families.
Sedge Fens: The marshes in the east of the Mereholt, around the town of Varn.
Sidd: The longest river of the Mereholt, which rises in Ost and forks at Lanterncross.
Silver glass-mark: A silver coin with a bead of green glass, struck by the Hollen Bank. 1 silver glass-mark is worth 5 salt bricks.
Tamber: The river that forms the border between the Vale of Siddow and Hollin's Reach.
Tidereckoning: The calendar of the Mereholt, counted from the Great Tide in TR 1.
Treaty of Varn: The treaty that ended the Salt War, signed in TR 318 in the marsh town of Varn.
Treaty of Varne: The treaty that brought Hollin's Reach into the realm, signed in TR 402 on the river island of Varne.
Unbroken Ground: The custom of Ost that forbids digging of any kind.
Unreckoned years: All the time before the Great Tide.
Vale of Siddow: The central farmland of the Mereholt, usually called the Vale.
Varn: The marsh town in the Sedge Fens, in the far east of the Mereholt.
Varne: The river-island city in the River Sidd, in the Vale of Siddow, home of the toll collectors.
Voice of Ost: The title given for one year to the winner of the long poem at the Reckoning of Verses.
